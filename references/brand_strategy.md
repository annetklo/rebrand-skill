# Brand strategy: territories and the carrying idea (Phase 1-2)

Method file for the /rebrand skill. Read this before building visual territories (Phase 1, step 3) and before writing concept notes (Phase 2). Input for everything below is the approved creative brief from Phase 0, especially the personality sliders from round 2 and the scope decision (evolution vs revolution) from round 1. Never build a territory before the brief gate is passed.

---

## 1. The one-idea principle

A lasting identity is carried by exactly one nameable idea, statable in one sentence.
The idea is usually found by interrogating the name, the merger, or the category, never by styling: Paula Scher opened the Windows 8 project with "Your name is Windows, why is your logo a flag?" and that question WAS the concept.
Sagi Haviv (Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv) formalized the test: appropriate (right personality, and the less it says the better), distinctive (someone can doodle it from memory after one or two exposures), simple (works at 16px and on a building).
The one-sentence test is mechanical: write the idea as one sentence; if the sentence needs "and" twice, it is two ideas, reject it.
Expect volume before the idea appears: Lindon Leader ran hundreds of comps before the FedEx arrow landed; three sketches without a carrying idea is not exploration, it is decoration.

### Documented cases

| Brand (year, designer) | The idea in one sentence | Why it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Citi (1998, Paula Scher, Pentagram) | The t in Citi carries the red Travelers umbrella. | The merger's protection promise lives inside the name itself. Sketched on a napkin in the first meeting, fee $1.5M; her defense "seconds to draw, 34 years to learn to draw it in seconds" is the argument for concept over labor. |
| Mastercard (2016, Michael Bierut, Pentagram) | The two overlapping circles ARE the brand, everything else comes off. | Identified the single equity element first, then subtracted toward it: bars, interlacing, and by 2019 even the word were removed. Reduction as concept, not restyle. |
| Chase (1961, Chermayeff & Geismar) | A deliberately abstract octagon around a fixed square center, designed to earn meaning instead of explaining it. | One of the first fully abstract US corporate marks. Proves an idea can be a stance rather than a picture: deliberate abstraction, chosen to unite two merged bank cultures under an unwieldy name, and left to acquire meaning through consistent use. |
| V&A (1989, Alan Fletcher, Pentagram) | Take away one leg of the A and let the ampersand give back the crossbar. | Three characters fuse into one object. The idea lives in a typographic collision, not an added symbol. |
| NASA worm (1975, Danne & Blackburn) | One continuous line with no crossbars in the A's, so the wordmark itself is engineered spaceflight. | The reproduction constraint (the meatball was near-impossible to print cleanly) became the idea. Retired 1992 for sentiment, revived 2020: equity can outlive an official retirement. |
| Windows 8 (2012, Paula Scher, Pentagram) | The logo is literally a window in perspective, not a flag. | Interrogate the name first: the strongest concept was already in it. One question reframed 25 years of flag logos. |
| I Love NY (1976, Milton Glaser) | The heart replaces the word, so the viewer completes the sentence. | A rebus sketched in a taxi during NYC's near-bankruptcy. Participation is why it spread: the viewer does the last step. |
| FedEx (1994, Lindon Leader, Landor) | In the white space between the E and the x sits a forward arrow. | Speed and precision smuggled into a custom Futura/Univers blend; invisible until seen, then unforgettable. Landed only after hundreds of comps, won 40+ design awards. |

### Idea classes (use as a generative checklist per territory)

1. **Name interrogation**: the idea is already in the name (Windows, V&A, NASA). First question of any concept round: what does the name literally say, and does the current mark say the same thing? For Mission Relearn: interrogate "Mission", "Re-", and "learn" separately before touching form.
2. **Equity reduction**: identify the one element people already recognize, subtract everything else (Mastercard). Only available in an evolution scenario; requires naming the equity element explicitly in the brief.
3. **Synthesis**: two things the brand unites, fused into one form (Citi, Chase). For MR this could be a fusion of education and technology, or of Mission Relearn and its method.
4. **Negative space**: the idea hides in the counterform (FedEx). High risk of gimmick; passes only if the mark also works for someone who never sees the hidden element.
5. **Rebus / participation**: the viewer completes the sentence (I Love NY). Works when the audience repeats or reproduces the mark themselves.
6. **Reproduction constraint as idea**: a hard constraint (one continuous stroke, single color, 16px favicon) becomes the formal concept (NASA worm). Useful when the brief's top 5 applications are unforgiving.

Every territory and every concept note must name its idea class. A direction that fits no class is styling, send it back.

## 2. Attribute-to-form translation

The personality sliders from the brief (discovery round 2) translate to form through this table. Grounding: angular marks measurably raise perceived competence, durability, and premiumness (Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services 2023); rounded marks raise perceived warmth and approachability (Jiang et al., JCR 2016); the Henderson/Giese/Cote line shows five measurable font characteristics (naturalness, elaborateness, harmony, flourish, weight) predict personality dimensions; warm hues pull toward energy and human proximity, cool hues toward institutional trust. Slider positions are tendencies, not binaries: a 70/30 position means the dominant cell wins but the minority cell may appear in one controlled place.

| Attribute | Form and geometry | Line and weight | Composition and symmetry | Color temperature | Typography |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| trustworthy | closed geometric forms on a visible construction grid | even, medium stroke, no modulation | symmetric, stable base wider than top | cool-leaning base, one warm accent max | geometric or grotesque sans, regular to medium |
| authoritative | angular, corner radius 0 or minimal | heavier stroke, solid fills over outlines | strict vertical symmetry, centered lockups | dark cool base (navy, ink), palette of 2-3 colors | tight caps or small caps, high x-height sans |
| warm | rounded corners and terminals (radius at least 15-20% of stroke width), open apertures | medium, slightly soft joins | gentle asymmetry allowed | warm hues carry the majority of accent use (coral, orange) | humanist sans, open counters, no light weights below 400 |
| playful | mixed corner radii, one rotated or bouncing element | varied but never thin | deliberate asymmetry, diagonal energy | saturated secondaries next to the core color | rounded sans or one quirky glyph detail, never more than one |
| sober | flat single-color mark, no gradients, no shadows, no 3D | one uniform weight | quiet, grid-aligned, generous margins | near-monochrome plus one accent | workhorse grotesque, moderate spacing, zero decorative detail |
| pioneering | forward diagonal or upward vector built into the mark | cut terminals, engineered joins | dynamic asymmetry pointing right or up | high-saturation accent on a neutral field | geometric sans with a single engineered oddity (cf. the worm's missing crossbars) |
| technical | modular construction with repeating unit (dot, line, cell) | precise equal stroke, sharp joins | grid explicitly visible or implied | cool palette, blue-gray neutrals | geometric or monospaced candidate in the mix |
| approachable | large counters, high aperture, exterior corners rounded | medium weight, never hairline | open spacing, nothing cramped | warm neutrals, high contrast for legibility | humanist sans, generous default letterspacing |
| premium / exclusive | angular, refined, small at scale | thin to light strokes, high stroke contrast | abundant whitespace, small mark relative to field | muted or dark palette, desaturated accents | high-contrast serif or light sans, wide tracking on caps |
| human | organic irregularity, humanist axis, drawn rather than constructed | modulated stroke (calligraphic memory) | asymmetric, hand-placed feel | warm-dominant palette | humanist or semi-serif, true italics available |
| decisive | condensed, angular, forward-leaning | heavy weight, tight counters | compact lockup, high density | dark base plus one hot accent | bold condensed sans, tight tracking |
| caring | circles, ellipses, enclosing or embracing forms | soft even stroke | contained, centered, protective | soft warm tints plus one saturated warm | rounded humanist sans, ample line height |
| idiosyncratic | one deliberate rule-break (one altered letter, one broken alignment), rest strictly disciplined | consistent except at the break | the break is the focal point, everything else quiet | conventional palette so the formal break carries alone | standard typeface with exactly one custom glyph |
| calm | horizontal emphasis, low contrast forms | light to regular, even | wide, symmetric, lots of air | low saturation, cool or neutral | light-to-regular sans, wide spacing, no bold in running text |

Usage rule: a territory is a coherent, internally consistent set of cells from this table. Pick the 3-4 attributes the brief scored highest, read their rows, and resolve conflicts explicitly (warm says rounded, authoritative says angular: the slider position between them decides which one owns geometry and which one gets a single controlled appearance, for example rounded forms but a strict centered composition). Never average cells into mush; choose.

## 3. Visual territories (Phase 1)

A territory is a conceptual direction, one level above a moodboard: it has a name, a carrying idea, and implications for type, color, form, and photography. Keep the fidelity ladder straight (distinction via SOAK and Chris Do's stylescape method): a **moodboard** is pulled reference work by others and answers only "are we pointing in the same direction?"; a **stylescape** is a curated composition with specific typefaces, color, and in-context mockups that functions as a visual contract; a **territory** is the conceptual layer both serve. In Phase 1 we present territories at moodboard fidelity; Phase 3 raises the chosen one to real design.

### How many, and how differentiated

Build 2-3, never more. Choose the differentiation scheme based on the brief:

- **Intensity axis** (The Futur's Hot / Medium / Mild): same strategy, three levels of daring. Use when the Phase 0 positioning is settled and the real question is how far to push. Maps naturally onto the evolution-vs-revolution answer: Mild = refine the current mark, Medium = same idea rebuilt, Hot = new carrying idea.
- **Concept axis** (Koto's parallel routes, e.g. their Glassdoor rebrand): each territory amplifies a DIFFERENT aspect of the strategy. Use when the brief itself has multiple credible readings (for MR plausibly: the educator reading, the technologist reading, the mission reading). Never mix the schemes in one round; it makes the choice incomparable.

### Naming pattern

Each territory gets a short evocative two-word working title that summarizes the carrying idea. Never "Option A", never adjective soup like "modern/sleek". Test: the name must work in the sentence "In the territory '...' the logo would ...". Good: "Warm Science", "Clear Institute", "The Spark", "Quiet Precision". Bad: "Direction 2", "Clean & Bold".

### Required contents per territory (all at EQUAL fidelity)

1. **Name**: two-word working title.
2. **The story in one sentence**: one sentence stating the carrying idea, passing the "and" test. Example format: "The mark is the orbit you step back into."
3. **Idea class**: which of the six classes from section 1.
4. **Moodboard**: 6-10 pulled references (other identities, texture, photography, type samples). Sourced via researcher subagents from the landscape scan, not generated imagery.
5. **Typography direction**: 1-2 named typeface candidates or a precise class ("geometric sans with humanist apertures, Cal Sans-adjacent"), taken from the matching cells in the translation table.
6. **Color mood**: palette tendency and warm:cool ratio, plus explicitly what happens to coral (dominant, accent, or replaced; the brief's "is coral sacred" answer decides the allowed range).
7. **Form language**: the specific cells from the translation table this territory commits to.
8. **Why this fits**: one line mapping to named brief answers ("scores 70/30 on the warm slider and on 'what should someone feel in 3 seconds': ...").
9. **Risk**: one honest line on what this direction sacrifices. Every territory has a cost; a territory without a named cost is a sales pitch.

The equal-fidelity rule is hard: an underbaked third option reads as a decoy and the client will notice. If you cannot bring a third territory to the same level, present two.

### Presenting for the choice (the Phase 1 gate)

1. Build one HTML gate page and open it as a local review surface (the HTML in the browser). Match the design language of the engagement's earlier exploration pages in <engagement-map>/ so the rounds read as one series: all territories side by side, identical layout per territory, the nine elements above visible for each.
2. After the client has seen the page, run one AskUserQuestion call: one option per territory, each option's description carrying the one-line story plus the risk line, phrased in the client's language. The client can always answer free-form (Other), including "none of these".
3. Record the choice and its motivation in the decision log. If the client rejects all territories, do NOT patch the losers; go back to the brief, ask which slider or answer the territories misread, and rebuild.

## 4. Concept note (Phase 2)

For the chosen territory (or the top two, if the gate ended in a tie) write one concept note each. The note is the contract for Phase 3: the form engine gets fed this note, not a loose description. Template, with client-facing fields (render them in the client's language when it is not English):

```markdown
## Concept: <working title>

**The idea in one sentence:** <one sentence, no double "and">

**Idea class:** <name interrogation | equity reduction | synthesis | negative space | rebus | reproduction constraint>

**Why this fits the brief:**
- <reference a concrete brief answer, e.g. "Round 1: 'the rocket may evolve, the movement must stay'">
- <reference a slider position, e.g. "warm↔technical at 70/30 warm: hence rounded terminals">
- <reference the 3-second question from round 2>

**Carry-through:**
- Symbol: <how the idea determines the form, incl. the chosen cells from the translation table>
- Wordmark: <how THE IDEA executes in the letters: case, spacing, and the surgical treatment from references/wordmark_typography.md. The note must state how the one idea executes at BOTH scales (full intensity in the mark, a precise echo or a deliberately argued alternative family in the wordmark). A concept note that bans the idea from the wordmark entirely fails review and returns to phase 2.>
- Color: <what the idea demands of the palette, role of coral>
- Language: <how the idea carries through in tone of voice and taglines, one example sentence>

**Haviv test:**
- Appropriate: <does it fit the personality, and does it avoid saying too much? Verdict + one sentence>
- Distinctive: <can someone sketch it from memory after seeing it once or twice? Verdict + one sentence>
- Simple: <does it work at 16px, on a building, in black and white? Verdict + one sentence>

**Kill criteria (this concept is dead if):**
1. <condition 1>
2. <condition 2>
3. <condition 3>
```

### Rules for kill criteria

Each concept gets exactly three, and they must be observable conditions testable in Phase 3-4, not vibes. Draw from this bank and sharpen per concept:

- "Dead if the mark no longer reads as <the idea> on a 16px favicon." (the Haviv simple test, made concrete)
- "Dead if a test subject cannot sketch it from memory after seeing it once." (the doodle test)
- "Dead if the idea only works with color: the black-and-white copy loses it." (color may support the idea, never carry it)
- "Dead if the custom letter detail cannot be built in the real font without breaking the word's overall shape."
- "Dead if the one-sentence idea turns out to need a second 'and' when the concept note is written out."
- "Dead if the hidden element (negative space) has to be explained for the brand to work."
- "Dead if the font license for the only suitable candidate exceeds <amount> and there is no equivalent alternative."

A concept that trips a kill criterion in Phase 3 dies there, without renegotiating the criterion. That is what the criteria are for: they were agreed before anyone fell in love with pixels.

### Lineage kills

The client can also kill a concept at any gate with an answer that reaches further back than the artifact on the table: "start over, back to before you proposed X" kills the entire concept LINEAGE, the concept plus every descendant variant built on it, not one variant. Record such a kill in the decision log as a lineage-kill, naming the root concept and the rounds it spans, but keep the artifacts: the client's favorites can resurrect "dead" work under a new reading, and a killed family may later turn out to be the source of the winning mark. After a lineage-kill, do not design again immediately; run the taste-recovery questions from SKILL.md first (what repelled, which earlier artifacts did land, must the mark carry the story), so the next round starts from a recovered direction instead of from more variants.

The Haviv test is the kill gate before anything reaches the form engine or image generation: a concept that fails appropriate, distinctive, or simple does not get variants, no matter how good the sketch looks. Remember that paid image renders (for example the showcase renders of the logo-generator skill, github.com/op7418/logo-generator-skill, if installed) cost money and run only on explicit request; a failed Haviv test never earns a render.

## 5. Hybrid concepts: the warning

At the concept gate the tempting answer is often "can we have both? Concept 1, but with the color of 2." Before agreeing to any merge, run these checks in order:

1. **Restate the hybrid in one sentence.** Write the merged idea as one sentence. If it needs "and" twice, it is two ideas wearing one trenchcoat: refuse the merge as a concept merge and say so plainly.
2. **Distinguish idea fusion from ingredient borrowing.** Merging two carrying ideas almost always kills both (a vault that is also an arrow is neither). Borrowing an ingredient is usually fine: the palette tendency of territory B applied inside territory A's idea, or B's typeface candidate, provided the borrowed cell does not contradict A's committed cells in the translation table. Name which of the two is being asked for before answering.
3. **Consistency check against the translation table.** If A committed to rounded/warm and the borrowed element from B is angular/cool, the hybrid contradicts itself and will read as indecision. Show the conflicting cells rather than arguing taste.
4. **Rerun the Haviv test on the hybrid.** A merge that was not tested is a new, untested concept. It gets its own concept note, its own kill criteria, and it competes on equal footing; it does not inherit the pass of its parents.
5. **Read the desire for a hybrid as a signal.** If neither concept convinced on its own, the fix is upstream, not in blending. Ask which specific quality is missing ("what is missing in concept 1 that you do see in 2?") and take that answer back to the brief or the territory round. Mastercard's lesson applies: the strong move is subtraction toward one idea, never addition of a second one.

If after the checks a legitimate ingredient-borrow remains, document it in the concept note under Carry-through with one line naming the donor territory, so Phase 3 knows the borrow was deliberate.
